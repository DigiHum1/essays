Ken’s Corner: The only known image of Charles Dickens in Broadstairs 
Here Ken reveals the remarkable story of a unique pen-and-ink sketch of Charles Dickens 
and friends on a night out in Broadstairs, the mysterious donor who left it at the town’s 
Dickens House (now a museum), its subsequent theft and miraculous (sort of) restoration 
In the late summer of 1840 Charles Dickens visited Broadstairs for six weeks whilst 
writing The Old Curiosity Shop. This was a ‘work hard, play hard’ holiday for the 28-year
old author who had invited a large party of friends to join him at the seaside. 
He spent his mornings alone with ink, quill and paper but sought out his companions 
every afternoon for swimming from the bathing machines, hikes into the surrounding 
countryside and outings to nearby beauty spots. Evenings were spent dining out, going to 
the Margate theatre, attending balls or staying at home to play parlour games. 
Among those with whom Dickens socialised that summer was 19-year-old Eleanor 
Picken, a talented artist staying in Albion Street with his friend Charles Smithson. Eleanor 
kept a detailed diary of the many hours she spent in Dickens’ company and after his 
death she used her daily entries to write an account of the holiday. One incident she 
described almost certainly happened in the Ranelagh pleasure gardens in St Peter’s. 
Whilst younger members of the Dickens’ party danced to the music of the military 
band, the writer strolled around the ornamental grounds chatting to friends. Suddenly he 
became aware he was being stalked by an unknown man who insolently stared at him 
whilst eavesdropping on his conversation. 
Writing in the Englishwoman’s Domestic Magazine in 1871, Eleanor said: ‘At last Dickens 
lost patience and turning, suddenly, confronted him, with “Pray, sir, are you a native of 
this place?” “N-no, sir,” stammered the individual. “Oh, I beg your pardon,” returned 
Dickens with elaborate politeness. “I fancied I could detect broad stares on your face.”  I 
need not say that the unhappy wight vanished into the shades of evening.’ 
After marrying naval officer Edward Christian in 1842, Eleanor pursued a career in art 
and achieved the distinction of having ten of her portraits hung at the Royal Academy. 
Fast forward to March 1959 – nearly 120 years after Dickens made his Broadstairs pun. 
Miss Gladys Waterer, secretary of the Broadstairs Dickens Fellowship and founder of the 
town’s Dickens Festival, was being interviewed by the Thanet Times about how her home 
had become Dickens’ inspiration for Betsey Trotwood’s cottage in David Copperfield. The 
reporter wrote: ‘Miss Waterer told me she often receives gifts of old prints and relics of 
Dickens from strangers. She showed me one picture that had been pushed into her hands 
as she stood at her front door, with the explanation it came from a Mr Abbott. 
‘When Miss Waterer unwrapped it she found a drawing of a group of strollers, one 
evidently being Dickens and another with a wide grin on his face.’ 
Miss Waterer went on to tell the journalist the previously unknown sketch portrayed the 
‘broad stares’ incident and that it bore the signature of its artist . . . Eleanor Christian, 
otherwise Miss Eleanor Picken, our young diarist from the 1840 holiday.  
So not only had Eleanor left a written account of the Ranelagh Gardens confrontation, 
she had also drawn the scene for posterity. Where the picture had lain for so many years 
we will never know but here it was turning up in Broadstairs all those years later. 
After Miss Waterer’s death in 1971, Dickens House passed to Broadstairs Council who 
opened it as a museum two years later. One of the prize exhibits was Eleanor Christian’s 
sketch – the only known image of Dickens in his beloved Broadstairs. 
Now roll on to June 27, 1989, and the Thanet Times carried another article, this time 
headlined: ‘Art thieves hit museum’.  The story reads: ‘The curator of Dickens House in 
Broadstairs, Mrs Joyce Smith, has appealed for the return of an irreplaceable painting 
stolen from the museum in Victoria Parade on Saturday afternoon. The sepia line-and
wash original by Eleanor Christian depicts a number of figures, including that of Dickens. 
Mrs Smith says the painting is unsaleable because it is so easily recognised.’ 
Sadly the painting has not been recovered and there were fears no copies of the drawing 
had ever been made. The image was lost for ever.  
Well, not quite. 
Someone working at the Dickens Fellowship HQ in London heard about the Broadstairs 
theft and recollected reading an article on Eleanor Christian in The Dickensian, the 
journal of the Dickens Fellowship. The story had featured the ‘broad stares’ incident so 
was it possible the author was aware of both the drawing’s existence and its whereabouts 
and had arranged for a photograph of the sketch to be taken? 
A search through the archives to May 1964 uncovered the article entitled, ‘Who Was The 
Lady? Mrs Christian’s reminiscences of Charles Dickens’. And miraculously the story was 
accompanied by a black-and-white photographic reproduction of the stolen picture. 
Soon a copy was winging its way back to Broadstairs where it was framed and hung in 
the back hall of Dickens House Museum. And there it still hangs today. 
In the 1989 Thanet Times story about its theft, Mrs Smith said the original sketch was 
‘sepia line-and-wash’. The Dickensian’s copy and therefore the one now hanging in 
Dickens House Museum is monochrome. So, using the power of my computer’s image 
editing software, I bring you . . . the pen-and-ink sketch with its original sepia wash.
