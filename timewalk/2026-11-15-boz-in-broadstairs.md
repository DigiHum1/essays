Appeared in Broadstairs Dickens Fellowship September 2019 newsletter 
It is early September 1837 and the small seaside resort of Broadstairs 
is playing host to two of Britain’s literary giants. 
At 12 High Street is Charles Dickens. Aged just 25, he is at the start 
of his writing career. Within a few years he will be considered by many 
to be the greatest novelist of his age. 
About 100 yards away at 5 Prospect Place1 is the Romantic poet 
William Wordsworth. The 67-year-old is at the height of his fame and 
Queen Victoria will soon bestow upon him the post of Poet Laureate. 
This is Dickens’ first holiday to Broadstairs and he and his young 
family are staying in a modest flint-and-brick cottage in the narrowest 
part of the street. He spends his mornings writing the latest 
instalment of his first novel The Pickwick Papers and afternoons 
playing on the sands with his baby son Charlie or going for long walks. 
At least one friend comes down from London and they spend a merry 
evening in the bar at The Albion Hotel. 
Dickens had intended spending a month here but he leaves after only 
two weeks following a break-in at his London home. 
Wordsworth’s lodgings are more substantial – a three storey end-of
terrace house with a balcony facing the sea. He is most famous today 
for his ‘I wandered lonely as a cloud’ poem about daffodils. During his 
week-long holiday he has arranged to spend time with his friend and 
fellow poet Samuel Rogers who is staying at the Albion Hotel2. 
Dickens is probably aware Wordsworth is staying nearby. Broadstairs 
is a small resort and news of a famous visitor would be on everyone’s 
lips. The poet’s presence is certainly reported in the Press3 and 
Wordsworth notes in his diary4: ‘This is a very pleasant, though not 
particularly amusing place; but the drives are agreeable; yesterday I 
went with Rogers to Margate and Kingsgate but it rained so we could 
not get out of the Carriage.’ 
Dickens and Wordsworth do meet face to face in 18435. Asked what 
he thought of the young novelist, Wordsworth is reported as saying: ‘I 
thought him a very talkative, vulgar young person – but I dare say he 
may be very clever. Mind, I don’t want to say a word against him, for I 
have never read a line he has written.’ 
Dickens is equally as dismissive: ‘Like him? Not at all. He is a 
dreadful old ass.’
